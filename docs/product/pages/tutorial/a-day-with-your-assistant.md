[Your first week](/docs/your-first-week) switches things on. This page is
about what an ordinary day looks like once they are. It follows one person,
the owner of a small garden-design studio, through a Tuesday, and it is
written so you can copy it hour for hour. Nothing here needs a setting you
have not already met; if a step relies on a connection you have not made yet,
the page says so.

The shape to hold onto is a loop, and [how your assistant thinks](/docs/how-your-assistant-thinks)
explains why it is a loop and not a list.

![Brief, work, capture, and a sharper brief tomorrow](diagram:day-loop)

## Morning: the brief arrives

At seven, before she is up, the **Daily brief** runs. If Telegram is
connected and "Send me the result" is ticked on the skill, it lands on her
phone; either way it is written to a page in her brain, so a missed morning
loses nothing.

The brief is short on purpose. Three things that matter today, chosen against
the goals page she corrected last week, not against the eleven entries in her
calendar. Then what is at risk: a client quote that was promised for Friday
and has not been started, and a nursery supplier whose reply has been sitting
unanswered since last Thursday.

Two things make that brief possible, and both are hers to grow. The calendar
and inbox come from [connecting Google](/docs/connect-google); without them
the brief works from the brain alone. The "what matters"
comes from the goals layer of her brain. A brief that keeps picking the wrong
three is a goals page that is thin, and the fix is one sentence in the
Terminal, covered in [grow the brain](/docs/grow-the-brain).

> Tip: if the brief does not arrive, the likeliest reason is that its schedule
> is still switched off. Every schedule ships off. The switch is on the
> [Skills](/docs/skills-page) page.

## Mid-morning: a draft, not a send

She opens the [Terminal](/docs/terminal-page) and asks for the supplier reply.

![The Terminal, where the day's conversation happens](shot:member-terminal)

```
Draft a reply to the nursery about the delayed bare-root order.
We need it by the 24th or the Harrison planting slips a fortnight.
Keep it friendly, we want to keep using them.
```

The draft lands close, because the brain already holds the Harrison job, the
supplier's page, and samples of how she writes. She changes two sentences,
copies it into her mail, and sends it herself. The assistant drafts; sending
is always hers. Then she tells it what she changed:

```
I sent this version. Learn my tone from the edits.
```

That one line is the whole of [teach it your voice](/docs/teach-it-your-voice).
Do it a handful of times and the drafts stop needing the two sentences.

Next she wants three figures from last month's plant costs in the proposal
she is writing. If the spreadsheet lives in a connected service, she asks and
it reads them. If it does not, she pastes the rows into the Terminal and asks.
Nothing on the mineral reaches out to the web or to a service that is not
connected, which is why [what you can connect](/docs/connections) is worth a
look in the first week.

## Afternoon: decisions go straight into the brain

On a call at two she decides two things: the studio's day rate goes up from
next month, and she is no longer taking on maintenance-only contracts. The
moment the call ends, before the next thing, she types:

```
Capture both of those. Day rate to $950 from 1 October, and no more
maintenance-only work. Put them where the brief will read them.
```

Ten seconds. The assistant writes the rate into the page that holds her
numbers and the boundary into her goals, and shows her what it wrote. This is
the step most people skip, and it is the one that makes the difference
between an assistant that knows you and one that knew you in week one. The
conversation does not survive the session; the page does.

Mid-afternoon she runs `/followup` by hand, because she has a gap and a
feeling something is slipping. It sweeps both directions: what she owes, and
what is owed to her. A client who was sent a quote nine days ago and has gone
quiet. A task she said she would do this week and has not started. It
surfaces; it does not act. Chasing is her call.

## Close: capture the session

At the end of the day, with the Terminal still open from the afternoon, she
types one command:

```
/capture
```

The assistant reads back over the day's conversation and proposes what should
have been written down but was not: a new person page for the nursery
contact she mentioned by name, a decision about the planting date, a task for
Thursday. It shows a diff for each and writes only what she confirms. Nothing
is written silently.

Then she closes the session. Tomorrow's Open is a new session, and that is
fine, because everything that mattered is now in a page.

> Careful: the one habit that undoes all of this is having a good long
> conversation, closing the window, and letting it evaporate. If it is worth
> knowing next week, capture it today. You cannot over-capture.

## Tomorrow: the brief is a little sharper

Wednesday's brief knows the new day rate and the new boundary. It does not
suggest quoting the maintenance enquiry that came in overnight; it flags it as
one to decline politely. Nobody re-typed anything. The memory grew, and the
mind read it.

That is the whole return on the work. A tool is the same on day one hundred
as on day one. This has been accumulating a hundred days of one studio.

## Build it in waves

If the day above sounds like more than you do now, that is because it is
three waves stacked, and you add them one at a time.

1. **Read only.** For the first week, the assistant reads and you act. The
   brief, `/followup`, questions in the Terminal. You learn what it knows and
   where the brain is thin.
2. **Drafting.** Once the answers are reliable, let it draft: replies,
   proposals, the awkward email. You still send everything. The edits teach
   it your voice.
3. **Scheduled.** When you would miss a thing if it stopped, schedule it. The
   brief, inbox triage, the weekly review. Deliver to Telegram so it reaches
   you where you are.

Read first, draft second, schedule third. The order is a trust ladder, and
[what it will not do](/docs/what-it-will-not-do) is the honest account of
the rungs. When a wave settles and you want a routine of your own, the five
switch-on cards and seven build cards in [starter recipes](/docs/starter-recipes)
are the next step, and [the CRIT method](/docs/the-crit-method) is how to
ask for anything the cards do not cover.
