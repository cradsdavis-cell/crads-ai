You can publish skills, pages, prompts and folders to your community's
[commons](/docs/share-with-your-community), where every member's mineral
offers them for install. This is the single most useful thing a host does,
and the easiest to do badly.

## Start empty

The catalogue ships with nothing in it, deliberately, and your instinct will be to
fill it before anybody arrives. Resist that for one cohort.

The reason is that you do not yet know what your community actually does with an
assistant, and a catalogue full of skills nobody asked for teaches your members
that the catalogue is decoration. Ten unused skills is a worse starting point than
none, because none is honest.

What to do instead: [run the first cohort](/docs/run-a-cohort-session), watch
what people ask their assistants for, and publish the third thing two different
people ask for. That skill will be used, because you watched it be needed.

## What is worth publishing

The test is whether it encodes something **specific to your community** that a
member could not reasonably work out alone.

Good candidates:

- **Your language.** If your community has a framework, a set of stages, a
  scorecard, a way of naming things: a skill that speaks it is worth more than any
  generic productivity recipe.
- **Your recurring work.** The report everyone files, the review everyone runs,
  the checklist everyone forgets.
- **Your standards.** How a proposal should look here. What a good client note
  contains.

Bad candidates, honestly:

- Anything already in the engine set. Every mineral already ships with inbox
  triage, the daily brief, follow-up chasing, week planning and the weekly
  review, plus capture and the connect machinery. Publishing your own version
  of any of them is noise.
- Anything that is really a document. If the value is information rather than
  action, publish it as a page or a folder, not a skill.
- Anything you have not run yourself. A published skill carries your name on it.

## Publish a page instead, sometimes

The commons carries **pages, prompts and folders** as readily as skills: your
playbook, your standards, your context. A member whose assistant knows how
your community thinks gives better answers on every question, not just the
ones a skill covers. If you are choosing between writing a skill and writing a
page, the page is usually the higher-leverage half hour.

## Publishing is gated, on purpose

Publishing lays your library out in the commons repository, and two gates run
before anything ships. Pages pass the same lint gate as everywhere else: a
failing page is skipped and named, never shipped silently. And a scrub refuses
anything secret-shaped (keys, tokens, a password assignment), naming the file
and line for every hit. Nothing is altered silently: you fix the file, then
publish again.

This is protection, not bureaucracy. What you publish lands where every member
can pull it, so anything you left inside it leaves your rock with it. The
classic catch is a test key pasted into a skill while you were building it and
forgotten. The scrub exists because that mistake is normal, not because you
are suspected of anything.

## What it looks like on their mineral

Three mechanics worth knowing before your first cohort, because each one gets
misread as a fault by hosts who do not.

**Delivery is a pull, not a push.** A member's mineral syncs your commons on
its own rhythm, so something you publish appears on their side within the
hour, not the second. A host who does not know this calls it broken in front
of the room.

**Everything arrives as an offer.** Nothing you publish installs itself on
anyone's machine, ever. Installing is the member's act, through their own lint
and sandbox gates, and what they install carries your community's name on it,
which is one more reason never to publish something you have not run yourself.

**What they installed is theirs.** Withdrawing something from the commons
stops new installs; it removes nothing anyone holds. Updates you publish are
offered the same way installs were: nobody has your fix until they take it,
so say so when you announce one.

## Members can refuse, and that is fine

A member decides what lands on their mineral. What you publish is offered, not
imposed, and a member who declines your catalogue is not doing anything wrong.
Removal on their side is painless and reversible on purpose: an offer a member
cannot cheaply walk back from is not really an offer.

Design for that. A skill that only works if everybody has it is a fragile skill.

## Keep it small on purpose

Review it each cohort and remove what nobody used. A catalogue with four things
people use is a resource. A catalogue with twenty things, four of which are used,
is the same resource with sixteen pieces of noise attached, and it costs a new
member ten minutes to work out which four matter.
