The software is not the hard part of hosting. Getting five busy people to change
how they work is the hard part, and it fails in a small number of predictable
ways.

## The one number that matters

Not how many members joined. **How many did [the interview](/docs/the-interview).**

A member who set up their mineral and never onboarded has an assistant that knows
nothing about them, which means every answer it gives is generic, which means they
conclude the product is generic and stop opening it. That is the whole failure
chain and it starts at the interview.

So if you track one thing, track that. And you track it by asking, because
under the [commons model](/docs/rocks-and-pebbles) the software gives you no
dashboard about anyone: members' machines are their own, and nothing about
what they do on them reaches you. That is the design, not a gap, and it makes
the habits below the whole of your visibility.

## Three failure modes

**1. Everyone set up, nobody onboarded.** The room went well, everybody left with
a working mineral, and two weeks later nothing has happened. Cause: the interview
is an hour and nobody blocked out an hour.

Fix it by making it an event. Not a homework instruction: a second session, in the
diary, where everyone does their own interview in the same room at the same time.
It works because it is scheduled, not because it is supervised.

**2. The catalogue as decoration.** You published nine skills before the first
cohort and nobody used any. Cause: they were guesses.

Fix it by publishing the third thing two members independently ask for, and
nothing before that: [curating for your community](/docs/curate-for-your-community)
makes the whole argument.

**3. The silent member.** One person stops using it and says nothing, because
saying nothing is easier than saying "I did not get it".

This is the one that needs you specifically, and it is why hosts exist rather than
members simply downloading software. Nobody tells a vendor they are stuck. They
will tell you. And since nothing in the software will flag them for you, the
two-week conversation below is not optional hygiene; it is the only detection
there is.

## The member who missed the room

There is always one, and the shape that works is a scheduled 30-minute
concierge call: you and them, doing the setup steps together, exactly as they
would have happened in the room ([run a cohort session](/docs/run-a-cohort-session)
lists the steps). Not a link and good luck. A member left to set up alone from
written instructions is a member who is probably three-quarters set up, and you
will spend more than thirty minutes finding out which quarter is missing.

## What to ask, and when

Two weeks in, individually, and not "how is it going".

Ask: **what did you ask it last week?** The answer is diagnostic. Someone who says
"I get the morning brief" is passively consuming. Someone who says "I asked it to
draft the thing and it got it half right so I fixed it" is actually using it, and
they are the person to learn from.

Then ask the person who cannot remember what they asked it whether they want
twenty minutes with you and their assistant. Most will say yes and most of those
become users.

One more that matters now: **is your backup done?** The one step only the
member can do (recording their passphrase off the box) is on
[back up and restore](/docs/back-up-and-restore), and a host who asks about it
once, early, saves someone a very bad day years later.

## What you are actually providing

Not software, and not hosting. Three things software cannot do:

- **Permission.** You have said this is worth an hour. That is why they gave it an
  hour.
- **Context.** Your pages and your skills make every member's assistant better at
  your domain than a generic one could be.
- **Somebody to tell.** The thing they will not raise with a vendor.

The first is spent once and the third compounds. Plan accordingly: your attention
in weeks two to four buys more adoption than anything you publish in week one.

## The question you will be asked

Someone will ask whether you can see their stuff, probably in front of the
others. The answer is two sentences, and it is structural rather than policy:
their assistant runs on a machine in their own hosting account that only their
own keys can open, and what you share with them is a repository their machine
reads, one way. You see nothing.

Do not improvise past that. The full versions are written down:
[what your community can see](/docs/what-your-community-can-see) for the
member-facing statement, and the [hosting agreement](/docs/hosting-a-rock) for
what you are and are not. Point at both. A host answering a privacy question
from memory in a room is a host generating a finding.

## What not to do

**Do not oversell it.** You will be tempted, because you are enthusiastic and
because you have seen it work for you. A member who was promised magic and got a
useful assistant is disappointed. One who was promised a useful assistant and got
one is delighted. Same product.

**Do not promise dates.** Give a gate, not a date: "after the next release". You do
not control the release.

**Do not stand between a member and a problem.** Send them to the source with
their bug: the software is headed to an open repository precisely so that
reports and fixes have somewhere public to live. A host who relays every
question becomes a bottleneck and a filter, and both of you end up with a worse
picture than if they had reported it directly.
