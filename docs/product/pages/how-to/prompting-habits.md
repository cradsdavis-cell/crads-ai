None of these is a trick. They are the habits of someone who briefs a capable
person well, applied to an assistant that reads your brain before it reads
your message. Most of them come from Nate Herk's teaching, and the phrases
that are his are marked; the reading is in
[where these ideas come from](/docs/where-these-ideas-come-from).
[The CRIT method](/docs/the-crit-method) is the longer form for a real brief;
this page is for the hundred small asks in between.

## Habit one: brief it like a manager, not a coder

"You are a manager of AI agents, not a coder" is Nate Herk's line, and it is
the mindset the rest depends on. You are not writing instructions for a
machine. You are handing work to a colleague who has read your files, so
write what you would say to that colleague: what the job is, who it is for,
what good looks like, and where to find what they need.

A two-person accountancy asking "do the client summary" gets a generic
summary. Asking "write the quarterly summary for Marsh & Co the way we sent
the last one, and read their page first" gets one that names the right
people and the right numbers. The second ask is not longer because it is
formal. It is longer because it says where to look.

## Habit two: say what to do, and why

"Give the why, not the prohibition" is also Nate Herk's, and it changes
drafts more than any other habit here. A bare "don't" is a wall with no
context, and the assistant will find the nearest gap in it. The reason lets
it generalise.

"Don't use jargon" produces a draft that avoids the three words you had in
mind and uses six others. "This goes to a client who finds tax stressful, so
plain words and short sentences" produces a draft written for that person,
including in the places you had not thought to forbid. State the goal, the
reader, and the constraint with its reason, in that order if you can.

## Habit three: make it prove it

"Make it prove it" is Nate Herk's phrase for a plain rule: before you accept
that something is done, ask for the evidence. An assistant that has read
sixty pages can be confidently wrong about the sixty-first, and the fix is
not distrust, it is a standing question.

```
Before you tell me this is done, point to what proves it: the page
you wrote, the figure you read, the message you found. If something
is not verified, say so plainly.
```

Ask it once per session and you will notice the answers change shape: fewer
smooth summaries, more "here is the line in the page that says so". Where
it cannot point at anything, that is your signal to check yourself before
acting. [What it will not do](/docs/what-it-will-not-do) is the honest list
of where that matters most.

## Habit four: match the effort to the job

There is no effort dial on your mineral; the words in your ask do the work.
A quick lookup and a careful piece of writing want different amounts of
thinking, and the assistant calibrates from how you phrase it.

"Quick pass: which three invoices are overdue?" gets a list. "Take your
time on this one: draft the letter to the landlord and think about what
could go wrong before you write" gets a draft with the problems named at
the top. Neither phrase is magic; they set the expectation the same way they
would with a person, and the difference in what comes back is real.

## Habit five: let it act once it has enough

Over-planning is its own way of never starting. If you have given the
context and the ask, let it go and do the thing, then correct the result.
Correcting a draft that exists is faster than describing the perfect draft in
advance, and the correction teaches the brain something the description
never would.

The exception is anything that leaves the mineral or changes something you
would not want changed. There the standing rule is the reverse: propose
first, then act on your yes. The assistant drafts and never sends; for
writing into your brain or your connected services, "show me before you
write it" is a sentence worth keeping in the workflow page.

## Habit six: say less as the brain grows

In the first week you will write long asks, because the brain is thin and
you are filling the gaps by hand. That is right for week one and wrong for
month three. As the pages fill, the context step of every brief becomes
"read the page", and the ask itself can shrink to what a colleague of six
months would need: "reply to Jo, keep it short".

If you find yourself still writing paragraphs in month three, the thing to
fix is the brain, not the ask. [Grow the brain](/docs/grow-the-brain) is the
page for that, and the test is simple: if you have explained it twice, it
belongs in a page.

## The instead-of table

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "Do the client summary." | "Write the Marsh & Co quarterly summary the way we sent the last one. Read their page first." |
| "Don't use jargon." | "This goes to a client who finds tax stressful, so plain words and short sentences." |
| "Is that done?" | "Point to what proves it. If something is not verified, say so." |
| "Find the overdue invoices." | "Quick pass: which three invoices are overdue?" |
| "Write the landlord letter." | "Take your time: draft the landlord letter, and name what could go wrong before you write." |
| "Here is everything about Jo again..." | "Reply to Jo about Thursday, keep it short." (once Jo has a page) |
| "Can you maybe have a look at the proposal if you get a chance?" | "Read the proposal page and tell me the three weakest claims." |

## Put the habit in the brain

Every habit above is a sentence you will otherwise type every day. The
better place for a sentence you type every day is the workflow layer of your
brain, `wiki/_layers/8-workflow.md`, which the assistant reads at the start
of every session. Say it once in the [Terminal](/docs/terminal-page):

```
Add to my workflow page: always point to the evidence before saying
something is done, and show me what you are about to write to a
page before you write it.
```

From then on the habit is the assistant's, not yours to remember. The same
page is where your voice lives, which is why [teach it your voice](/docs/teach-it-your-voice)
sends you to the same file, and the way to find out which habits are already
there is to ask: `/explain` reads the mineral and answers from what it finds.
